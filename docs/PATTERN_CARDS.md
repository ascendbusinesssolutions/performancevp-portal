# PerformanceVP Online Pattern Cards

**Status:** Draft v0.1, 23 September 2026, for Michael's review. The 29 online editions of the Intervention Design Library's patterns (26 sub-dimension patterns and 3 trip-wire remediations; the 3 DLP patterns are not online). Written to Online Recommendations Specification Parts 4.1 and 4.3 and the voice rules in `PORTAL_COPY_SPEC.md`. This file is the source for the `ref_pattern_cards` seed; each card is versioned with the file.

**What a card is, and is not.** A card tells a client which proven pattern most likely fits what its measurement shows, what the pattern involves in outline, what movement is plausible and what tends to go wrong. It does not validate the cause, tailor the design to the unit, set success criteria or plan implementation. Those are Intervention Design. Every card ends with the same fixed offer, held once in the copy module: *Want it designed for this unit? Intervention Design validates the cause, tailors the fix and sets the measurement plan. Your organisation still implements.*

**Fields.** `addresses` (one or two sentences), `signs` (3 to 5), `outline` (4 to 6 steps, no timelines or owners), `forms` (the Library's variants, one line each), `movement` (indicative range, in points on the sub-dimension; an evidence-informed estimate assuming competent implementation, never a forecast), `projection` (whether the index projection is shown: yes where the range applies to the whole sub-dimension), `pace`, `could_show`, `evidence` (rating and a one-line basis), `wrong` (3), `watch` (cross-effects and trip-wire adjacency). Conditional sentences (the trip-wire adjacency line, the named process, team concentration, the inflation-check result) are switched on by the recommendations package per Copy Specification R13 and are not repeated here.

**Withheld from every card** (Recommendations Specification 4.1): the variant selection tables, the root-cause validation method, the case sketches, the effect-estimation adjustments and overlap modelling.

## Capability

### C1-P1 · Coverage gap in a role family

- **addresses.** A role family lacks people with a required skill at enough depth. The gap may be urgent or slow-building, and the fix is to buy, build or borrow the skill.
- **signs.**
  - Demand for a skill has outgrown hiring and development
  - A new skill, often technology-driven, became critical without investment
  - Turnover has hollowed out a role family without backfill
  - No skills framework was ever built, so the gap is structural
- **outline.**
  1. Size the gap against the role family's skills framework. Separate urgent from medium-term.
  2. Buy where the gap is acute and scarce inside. Build where it is medium-term and adjacent capability exists. Borrow where it is bounded or a bridge.
  3. Sequence by urgency: buy first, build in parallel, borrow to cover.
  4. Make managers create the space to apply the new skill. Set application milestones.
  5. Build or refresh the framework where it is missing or stale.
- **forms.** Urgent buy-led: recruitment, with build running alongside. Build-led: a development programme, with borrowing to bridge. Framework rebuild: build the framework first, then act.
- **movement.** +5 to +12 buy-led; +4 to +10 build-led; +6 to +14 where the framework is rebuilt (the rebuild improves the measurement before capability moves).
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow. Buy-led shows first.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** A. Strategic HR and training-effectiveness research.
- **wrong.**
  - Building when the gap is urgent enough to require buying
  - Buying without a retention plan; hires leave within 18 months
  - Training without manager reinforcement; skill gained, not applied
- **watch.** Pay equity, where hires arrive on materially higher pay than incumbents. Rising capability puts pressure on clarity and tools, because capable people expect the systems to use what they can do.

### C1-P2 · Leadership behavioural capability

- **addresses.** The unit's leaders lack the behavioural skills their level needs, and it shows in their teams before it shows in them.
- **signs.**
  - Promotion on technical excellence without behavioural development
  - Behavioural expectations never written down, so leaders cannot calibrate
  - Hard conversations avoided, or teams micromanaged
  - Leadership enablement low in the affected teams
- **outline.**
  1. A behavioural diagnostic against a defined framework, with individual and cohort reports.
  2. A cohort programme on the shared weaknesses, paired with individual coaching on personal priorities.
  3. The leaders' own managers reinforce and observe, or the change is neither noticed nor rewarded.
  4. Re-measure behaviour and read it against team engagement, turnover and enablement.
- **forms.** Cohort programme: the gap runs across five or more leaders. Targeted individual: concentrated in one to three. Behavioural framework: none exists yet, so build it first.
- **movement.** +4 to +8 on the behavioural part of skill coverage for the cohort; +5 to +10 on leadership enablement in the affected teams, which moves first.
- **projection.** No; the range applies to a component and to affected teams.
- **pace.** Mixed. Team perception can move within a quarter or two; the skill measure reads annually.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read for enablement; annual for skill coverage.
- **evidence.** A. Leader development, 360 feedback and coaching research.
- **wrong.**
  - Coaching without the boss's involvement; the boss keeps rewarding the old behaviour
  - Generic leadership training instead of targeted behaviour change
  - Putting off the role-change decision when development has failed
- **watch.** Fairness, if development is uneven across leaders. Usually lifts psychological safety, enablement and engagement in the affected teams.

### C2-P1 · Knowledge structure and accessibility

- **addresses.** Knowledge is uneven, undocumented, held by a few people or impossible to find, so work routes through asking.
- **signs.**
  - Long-tenured people hold what others need; a departure would leave a cliff
  - Documentation sparse, stale or unfindable
  - New starters take nine months or more to become competent
  - Regulatory or product knowledge delivered as events and forgotten
- **outline.**
  1. Map what the unit knows and needs, by role family, process and domain, and who holds it.
  2. Rank the risks: single points of failure; stale, contradictory or missing documentation.
  3. Fix each structurally: cross-training and handover for single points; owned, refreshed documentation; a knowledge baseline for new starters, competent at 90 days.
  4. Reinforce regulatory and product knowledge with spaced short checks, not annual events.
  5. Recognise contribution and protect the time to document.
- **forms.** Single point of failure: documentation and cross-training. Onboarding accelerator: build the baseline. Regulatory currency: spaced reinforcement and checks.
- **movement.** +4 to +9; +6 to +12 for the onboarding accelerator, which catches every new starter.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Knowledge management and spaced-retention research.
- **wrong.**
  - Buying a platform and assuming people fill it
  - Documentation nobody can find
  - Experts asked to document with no time or recognition
- **watch.** Compliance exposure where the knowledge gap touches regulated work. Often lifts collective intelligence and learning velocity.

### C2-P2 · Structured knowledge transfer

- **addresses.** The knowledge that has to move is judgement and know-how that documentation cannot capture. It transfers person to person, or not at all.
- **signs.**
  - A knowledge holder is leaving, or at risk of leaving
  - Junior staff have the documents but not the judgement
  - Mentoring happens ad hoc and does not stick
- **outline.**
  1. Name the tacit knowledge at risk and who holds it.
  2. Pair holders with recipients under defined goals, protected time and milestones.
  3. The expert thinks aloud; the recipient practises with feedback; support fades as competence builds.
  4. Protect and recognise the expert's transfer time.
  5. Capture as a by-product whatever turns out to be documentable.
- **forms.** Succession transfer: intensive pairing against a departure date. Cognitive apprenticeship: modelling, then faded support. Applied mentoring: pairing on real work using the documents.
- **movement.** +4 to +9.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Tacit knowledge, mentoring and apprenticeship research.
- **wrong.**
  - Mentoring with no structure, goals or time, so it does not happen
  - The expert's transfer time crowded out by delivery
  - No plan to fade support, so the recipient stays dependent
- **watch.** Low trip-wire risk. Often lifts learning velocity and collective intelligence; pairs with knowledge structure where documentation surfaces.

### C3-P1 · Performance management rigour reset

- **addresses.** Ratings are inflated and uncalibrated, so real talent density is unknown and weak performers are carried. The first effect is a more honest picture, which may read lower before it improves.
- **signs.**
  - Most people rated in the top two bands
  - Each manager applies a personal standard; no cross-manager calibration
  - Band descriptions vague or absent
  - Kind ratings mean fewer hard conversations
- **outline.**
  1. Compare the rating distribution with a defensible one and find the managers who inflate.
  2. Write specific, observable anchors per band per role family.
  3. Calibrate across managers at the rating cycle.
  4. Act on the low band once it is visible: development where viable, managed exits where not.
  5. Develop the inflating managers in rigour and difficult conversations.
- **forms.** Diagnostic only: calibrate and see. Full cycle: diagnose, calibrate, act, develop managers. System redesign: the rating scales, process and incentives themselves.
- **movement.** +4 to +10, after the score has first dropped to an honest level.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow. Density takes 12 to 24 months to move.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Performance management and rater-error research.
- **wrong.**
  - Treating calibration as the fix; it is the diagnosis
  - Managers drifting back to inflation next cycle
  - Calibration without psychological safety, which damages engagement
- **watch.** Fairness, if calibration looks arbitrary. Pay equity, where replacement hires command higher pay.

### C3-P2 · Selective talent injection

- **addresses.** Talent density is low and the measurement is sound. The unit needs more high-calibre people in the roles that move its output most.
- **signs.**
  - Ratings passed the inflation check and density is still low
  - Pivotal roles filled by capable rather than strong performers
  - Strong people exist elsewhere in the organisation
- **outline.**
  1. Confirm the low density is real, not an inflated picture just corrected.
  2. Identify the pivotal roles, the few where a strong performer changes unit output.
  3. Inject: targeted senior hiring, selective internal promotion, strategic redeployment.
  4. Retain the strong performers already there, or the gain is a wash.
  5. Ready the environment: tools, decision rights and leadership that a strong hire expects.
- **forms.** Pivotal hire: targeted recruitment. Selective promotion: genuine high performers ready now. Redeployment: strong people moved from elsewhere.
- **movement.** +4 to +10, concentrated where the injection lands.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow. Senior hiring carries lead time.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Talent density and pivotal-role research.
- **wrong.**
  - Strong hires arriving into an environment that frustrates them, then leaving
  - Adding density while losing it
  - Hiring senior people without a pivotal-role focus
- **watch.** Pay equity, where injections arrive on higher pay. Fairness, where external hires look like a block on internal progression. Readiness in clarity, tools and enablement matters before the hire lands.

### C4-P1 · Expertise clarity

- **addresses.** People do not know who knows what. Expertise is siloed, invisible or duplicated, and problems get solved alone that a colleague has already solved.
- **signs.**
  - A large, remote or hybrid team with low visibility of each other's expertise
  - A new or recently reshaped team
  - Specialist knowledge that surfaces only by accident
  - Rigid boundaries between teams
- **outline.**
  1. Map the team's real expertise together: qualifications, informal capabilities, project history.
  2. Keep a searchable directory in the existing collaboration platform, with an owner and a quarterly refresh.
  3. Install cross-exposure: short talks, spotlights, paired work across specialisms.
  4. Set norms for finding expertise, so the directory is how work is routed.
  5. Where the unit spans teams, add communities of practice.
- **forms.** Mapping only: a small, stable team. Cross-exposure: a new, merged or remote team. Cross-boundary: the gap is between teams.
- **movement.** +5 to +10. Clarity moves first, flow follows.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Moderate.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** A. Transactive memory research.
- **wrong.**
  - A directory nobody maintains, stale within 90 days
  - Mapping without changing how work is routed
  - Mandated sharing forums, which produce attendance rather than knowledge
- **watch.** No direct trip-wire risk. Usually lifts knowledge, learning velocity and engagement.

### C5-P1 · Learning system redesign

- **addresses.** The team learns slowly. Lessons do not transfer, new starters ramp slowly, and skill development happens as events rather than continuously.
- **signs.**
  - Retrospectives absent, perfunctory or producing no action
  - Each new starter reinvents the path
  - Managers focused only on delivery; no protected learning time
  - Failure treated as fault, not data
- **outline.**
  1. Find the learning bottleneck: onboarding, post-incident, or skill transfer.
  2. Install retrospectives at the work's rhythm, with lessons tracked to named actions.
  3. Systematise onboarding: expectations at 30, 60 and 90 days, a buddy, skill milestones.
  4. Build deliberate practice on the two or three skills that matter most.
  5. Protect learning time, and have managers defend it.
- **forms.** Retrospective focus: the team has time but not the practice. Onboarding acceleration: high churn or recent growth. Deliberate practice: specific skill weaknesses limit performance.
- **movement.** +5 to +10.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow to confirm. Practices install in weeks; the measure reads annually.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** A. Team learning, deliberate practice and learning-from-failure research.
- **wrong.**
  - Retrospectives that produce no action
  - Learning time eaten by delivery within weeks
  - Retrospectives sliding into blame where safety is low
- **watch.** Low psychological safety caps this pattern; address it first or alongside. Usually lifts engagement and collective intelligence.

### C5-P2 · External capability injection

- **addresses.** The team needs to learn something it has no seed of inside: a method, a technology, a domain. It cannot learn its way there alone, so external capability seeds it and transfers it.
- **signs.**
  - A capability nobody in the unit holds at all
  - Self-teaching attempts that have failed
  - A step change in what the work requires
- **outline.**
  1. Define the capability precisely and confirm there is no internal seed to grow.
  2. Bring the external capability in to transfer, not to deliver: an embedded practitioner working alongside the team with explicit transfer obligations.
  3. Structure the transfer: paired work, the expert as a working model, practices the team writes down as it learns.
  4. Plan the exit from the start, so capability forms inside and dependency does not.
- **forms.** Embedded expert: a practitioner alongside with transfer obligations. Partner transfer: a partner seeds the capability and exits to plan. Coach-led: coaching against an expert model after self-teaching failed.
- **movement.** +5 to +12 where transfer is real and the exit is managed; less where the outsider does the work.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow to confirm.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Absorptive capacity and knowledge-transfer research.
- **wrong.**
  - The outsider delivers instead of transferring; the team never learns
  - No managed exit; the temporary expert becomes permanent
  - Seeding a capability with no learning system to keep it growing
- **watch.** Watch the optics of contractor pay beside incumbents. Usually lifts skill coverage and collective intelligence.

## Motivation

### M1-P1 · Sub-population deep-dive

- **addresses.** The unit's engagement average looks acceptable, but one group inside it is in trouble and the average hides it.
- **signs.**
  - One team's scores sit well below the rest
  - One manager's team accounts for most of the drag
  - A recent event hit one group harder than others
  - Turnover or absence concentrated in one place
- **outline.**
  1. Cut the engagement results by team, tenure and role family to find the group.
  2. Listen to that group specifically: what demands are excessive, what resources are missing.
  3. Respond to the specific cause: workload, manager, recognition, role design, career path. Not a general engagement programme.
  4. Watch the group's turnover and absence monthly before the next read.
  5. Use the team's manager as implementer, unless the manager is the cause.
- **forms.** Manager-specific: the drag is one manager's team. Workload: capacity rebalance. Recognition: redesign it. Career pathing: career conversations and internal mobility.
- **movement.** +3 to +8 for the affected group; +2 to +5 on the unit, depending on the group's size.
- **projection.** Yes, on the unit-level figure.
- **pace.** Fast.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read; the quarterly pulse may show it first.
- **evidence.** A. Job demands and resources research.
- **wrong.**
  - Acting on the unit average when the problem is concentrated
  - Listening once with no follow-through, which erodes trust further
  - Treating engagement as the cause rather than the effect
- **watch.** A deep-dive sometimes surfaces a fairness or pay-equity issue; treat it as a critical finding. Often overlaps with resource adequacy and leadership enablement.

### M1-P2 · Confidence and efficacy lift

- **addresses.** Engagement is acceptable but the team has stopped believing it can succeed, usually after a visible failure, a run of misses, or goals set beyond reach.
- **signs.**
  - A recent visible failure
  - Goals disconnected from capability or resources, so misses keep coming
  - Leader communication that dwells on threats and shortfalls
  - Team-confidence items well below the engagement items
- **outline.**
  1. Recalibrate one or two key goals to a genuine reach: hard, attainable with focus.
  2. Engineer visible near-term wins and resource the team to land them.
  3. Show the team peers who overcame the same kind of problem.
  4. Redesign leader communication toward progress and capabilities applied, without hiding real problems.
  5. Add retrospectives that surface what is working, not only what failed.
- **forms.** Post-failure: near-term wins and an honest account. Stretched goals: recalibrate them. Communication: wins exist but are not reinforced.
- **movement.** +3 to +7 on the team-confidence part of engagement.
- **projection.** No; the range applies to a component.
- **pace.** Fast. Among the most responsive patterns.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read; the quarterly pulse may show it first.
- **evidence.** A. Collective efficacy and feedback research.
- **wrong.**
  - Manufactured wins the team reads as cosmetic
  - Communication the reality does not support
  - A burst of wins, then the old pattern
- **watch.** Confident teams need clear authority; pairs with decision rights. Enables the risk-taking that learning requires.

### M2-P1 · Structural psychological safety

- **addresses.** People do not speak up because something structural punishes it: how incidents are reviewed, how the speak-up channel responds, how performance management treats disclosed errors.
- **signs.**
  - Incident review assigns individual blame before it examines the system
  - Speaking up was visibly punished recently
  - Disclosed errors count against ratings
  - A speak-up channel nobody trusts
- **outline.**
  1. Identify which structure chills disclosure.
  2. Redesign it: incident review that separates honest error from at-risk and reckless behaviour; a rebuilt speak-up channel with visible fair outcomes; disclosure data kept out of evaluation.
  3. The leader handles the first few cases visibly under the new approach.
  4. Communicate the approach and its reasons, repeatedly.
- **forms.** Just culture: incident-review redesign. Speak-up redesign: channel and response protocol. Separation: decouple disclosure from rating.
- **movement.** +5 to +10. The structural change is visible quickly.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Fast, confirmed at the half-yearly read.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** A. Psychological safety and just culture research.
- **wrong.**
  - The process rewritten and the first cases handled the old way
  - Redesigning around a problem leader without addressing the leader
  - Reading a rise in reported errors as failure; it usually means success
- **watch.** Fairness, if similar cases visibly get different responses. Usually lifts learning velocity, conflict health and engagement.

### M2-P2 · Leadership practice and psychological safety

- **addresses.** People do not speak up because of how leadership responds to bad news, dissent or error. The lever is leadership practice, not a process.
- **signs.**
  - Bad news met with a sharp, dismissive or escalating response
  - Dissent visibly punished
  - Leaders who never admit error or uncertainty
  - Safe most of the time, unsafe under pressure
- **outline.**
  1. A targeted 360 on the behaviours that support safety, weighted to direct reports.
  2. Individual coaching against specific behavioural goals from the 360.
  3. Real-time feedback from a trusted peer or coach, so self-perception does not drift from the team's.
  4. The leader's own manager reinforces the change, or it reverts under pressure.
- **forms.** High-stakes only: the stress response. Pattern-wide: habitual, not stress-bound. With role redesign: the role itself is too demanding.
- **movement.** +4 to +8 in the affected team. Depends on the leader's receptivity.
- **projection.** No; the range applies to affected teams.
- **pace.** Moderate.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** A. Leader behaviour and safety research; coaching research.
- **wrong.**
  - Coaching a leader whose incentives reward the unsafe behaviour
  - Coaching without the leader's own manager involved
  - Declaring success at the announcement
- **watch.** Where leadership behaviour drove fairness perceptions, the fairness trip-wire may move too. Usually lifts engagement and enablement in the affected team.

### M3-P1 · Autonomy-supportive redesign

- **addresses.** Work is experienced as controlled from outside. People comply rather than take initiative.
- **signs.**
  - Roles specify the how, not just the what
  - Managers manage outputs by directing inputs
  - Performance management rewards process compliance
  - Recognition rewards conformity, not initiative
- **outline.**
  1. Audit role design for task-level over-specification; keep what compliance, safety and integration require, drop the rest.
  2. Make outcomes clear and free the method.
  3. Develop managers in autonomy-supportive practice: ask before telling, explain constraints, offer choice within them.
  4. Redesign recognition to reward improvement and initiative.
  5. Make the real constraints explicit, minimal and explained.
- **forms.** Job redesign: over-specified roles. Manager practice: roles fine, managers directing. Recognition redesign: the system rewards the wrong thing.
- **movement.** +4 to +9.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow. Closer to disposition than mood.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** A. Self-determination theory and job design research.
- **wrong.**
  - Autonomy in name only: told they have it, still directed
  - Autonomy without competence; pair with skill or learning work
  - Manager training while the role stays over-specified
- **watch.** Fairness, where autonomy is granted unevenly without a reason. Usually lifts engagement, purpose and learning.

### M4-P1 · Purpose translation and personal connection

- **addresses.** People cannot connect their day-to-day work to an outcome that matters. Corporate purpose exists and never reaches the unit.
- **signs.**
  - Purpose statements abstract and never translated
  - Leaders rarely say why the work matters
  - Recognition celebrates delivery, not the outcome it served
  - A recent change cut the work off from the story it belonged to
- **outline.**
  1. Translate purpose into a unit-specific account of why its work matters, built together.
  2. Have leaders say it, regularly, in ordinary communication. The change is repetition, not a launch.
  3. Redesign recognition to name outcomes, not only outputs.
  4. Expose the unit to customers and downstream results.
  5. Allow job crafting where roles permit.
- **forms.** Translation: purpose exists, not translated. Reconnection: a change broke the narrative. Outcome exposure: the unit is far from the outcomes it serves.
- **movement.** +3 to +7.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow. The slowest of the motivation levers.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Meaningful-work and job-crafting research.
- **wrong.**
  - A purpose statement nobody refers to again
  - Leader articulation that is not believed, which lowers the score
  - Cherry-picked outcome stories
- **watch.** Fairness, where purpose communication implies all work is meaningful when some is not. Usually lifts engagement and retention.

## Opportunity

### O1-P1 · Decision-rights reset

- **addresses.** People do not know who decides. Decisions escalate, or get made twice. Rights are undocumented, or documented and not followed.
- **signs.**
  - Rights evolved by habit and personality
  - Documented, never communicated
  - A manager overrides delegated calls
  - Approval thresholds contradict the delegation
- **outline.**
  1. Sample 15 to 25 recent decisions across operational, tactical and strategic.
  2. For each, set who recommends, inputs, decides, performs and owns.
  3. Fix the cause, not the document: re-communicate, raise thresholds, confront the override, or document with the rights-holders present.
  4. Test against the next 5 to 10 real decisions and adjust.
  5. Check quarterly, or it drifts back.
- **forms.** Communication: documented and correct, but not known. Threshold: approval thresholds undermine delegation. Manager override: one person overrides; address directly with leadership practice. Build from scratch: undocumented; a full build with the rights-holders present.
- **movement.** +5 to +12. Among the more measurable patterns.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Moderate.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** B. Decision-rights frameworks and role-ambiguity research.
- **wrong.**
  - Re-communicating when the cause is an override
  - Fixing the document, not the lived experience
  - Specifying every decision; a thick document, the same vague reality
- **watch.** Fairness, where new rights concentrate in a few roles. Usually lifts autonomy and leadership enablement.

### O1-P2 · Cascade alignment

- **addresses.** People cannot see how the unit's work serves the strategy. Each layer reads the strategy differently, and teams carry many loosely connected objectives.
- **signs.**
  - Strategy communicated but not cascaded
  - Goal-setting treated as compliance
  - Eight objectives per team, loosely connected
  - The cascade broken at one layer
- **outline.**
  1. Trace 5 to 10 team objectives back to the enterprise's and find the breaks.
  2. Rebuild so each layer translates the one above into a few focused objectives.
  3. Limit each layer to three to five.
  4. Make the cascade visible and traceable; visibility is half the lever.
  5. Review it each quarter as strategy moves.
- **forms.** Objective discipline: the goal system exists in name only. Strategy volume: reduce, do not add. Cascade rebuild: rebuild from the broken layer.
- **movement.** +4 to +10 on the cascade part of clarity.
- **projection.** No; the range applies to a component.
- **pace.** Moderate to slow. A full strategy cycle to embed.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Strategy maps and goal-setting research.
- **wrong.**
  - The goal system as theatre
  - A one-off rebuild that goes stale within two cycles
  - Volume imposed from above that the unit cannot fix
- **watch.** No trip-wire risk. Usually lifts purpose and autonomy.

### O2-P1 · Tool rationalisation and integration

- **addresses.** Too many tools, tools that do not connect, or a critical tool missing. The cost shows as time lost, manual handoffs and errors.
- **signs.**
  - Overlapping tools, none done well
  - Data moved by hand between systems
  - A class of work with no tool for it
  - Centrally mandated tools that do not fit the work
- **outline.**
  1. Inventory the tools: what is used, for what, by whom, what overlaps.
  2. Map where people switch tools or move data by hand.
  3. Rationalise: pick one where two do the same job, and migrate.
  4. Integrate where the gap is structural; where that is prohibitive, design the manual step to minimise friction.
  5. Fill critical gaps with the right tool and the practices to use it.
  6. Support adoption: training, champions, manager reinforcement.
- **forms.** Rationalisation-led: consolidate sprawl. Integration-led: right tools, disconnected. Gap-fill: acquire and adopt the missing tool.
- **movement.** +5 to +12.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Moderate. Single gaps show faster than broad rationalisation.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** B. Sociotechnical systems and technology adoption research.
- **wrong.**
  - Replacing a tool without redesigning the process
  - Big-bang rollout without support; productivity dips and reversion follows
  - Ignoring the unofficial tools people already use
- **watch.** Basic conditions, where tool gaps create unsafe or non-compliant work. Usually lifts process and collaboration friction.

### O2-P2 · Information access redesign

- **addresses.** The tools are adequate but people cannot get at the information they need. Data is locked, scattered or in no usable form.
- **signs.**
  - Data exists but access is withheld
  - Reports assembled by hand from several sources
  - Sources that contradict each other, so none is trusted
- **outline.**
  1. Map the information the unit's core work needs, for which decisions and tasks.
  2. Find the bottlenecks: locked data, manual assembly, missing or untrusted reporting.
  3. Fix access structurally: provisioning, one trusted source, self-serve reporting for recurring needs.
  4. Build the views around the questions the work asks, not the data available.
  5. Support adoption so the new access is used, not worked around.
- **forms.** Access provisioning: unlock the data. Self-serve reporting: build the recurring views. Single source: establish the trusted one and retire the rest.
- **movement.** +4 to +10.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Moderate. Provisioning shows fast; platform work slower.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** B. Information-processing and data management research.
- **wrong.**
  - Reporting built around available data, not the questions asked
  - Access without governance; a friction problem becomes a data-risk problem
  - A single source that does not retire the old ones
- **watch.** Compliance, where access touches sensitive data; provision with governance. Usually lifts process.

### O3-P1 · Targeted bottleneck redesign

- **addresses.** One step in a process creates rework, delay or a queue. The rest of the process may be fine.
- **signs.**
  - A handoff between teams that keeps failing
  - An approval at the wrong level
  - A tool that forces a workaround
  - One role processing a queue that backs up
- **outline.**
  1. Find the constraining step from process data or the friction audit.
  2. Redesign that step only: remove, automate, parallelise or re-sequence.
  3. Adjust the step after it; a removed bottleneck often moves to the next-tightest.
  4. Measure cycle time, rework and queue before and after.
  5. Give the step an owner, or the redesign decays.
- **forms.** Handoff: redesign the failing handoff. Approval threshold: change the threshold, with decision rights. Tool constraint: with tool rationalisation. Capacity constraint: with capacity rebalance.
- **movement.** +6 to +14.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Fast. Among the fastest and most measurable patterns.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** A. Theory of constraints, lean and process-mining research.
- **wrong.**
  - Redesigning the whole process
  - Moving the bottleneck downstream
  - No baseline, so the bottleneck is opinion
- **watch.** Basic conditions, if the redesign strips job content without redesigning roles. Interacts with resource adequacy.

### O4-P1 · Demand management and capacity rebalance

- **addresses.** Sustained capacity stress: overtime, a growing backlog, burnout signs, turnover in the constrained roles.
- **signs.**
  - Demand grew faster than capacity, with no re-prioritisation
  - The unit accepts work it should decline or route elsewhere
  - Headcount cut without cutting scope
  - One role family constrained while others have slack
- **outline.**
  1. Compare work asked for with sustainable capacity and locate the imbalance.
  2. Manage demand first: explicit priorities, decline-or-defer rules, routing.
  3. Invest in productivity: tools, process, automation.
  4. Expand capacity only where the gap is structural and other levers are spent.
  5. Have leaders protect sustainable pace, so the gap does not reopen.
- **forms.** Demand filter: discipline on what is accepted. Productivity investment: with tools and process. Capacity expansion: headcount, last. Internal rebalance: move capacity between role families.
- **movement.** +4 to +10.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Mixed. Filtering shows fast; hiring is slow.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** B. Job demands and resources and burnout research.
- **wrong.**
  - Adding capacity into unfiltered demand; same gap, higher cost
  - Filtering without leadership cover; overridden within a quarter
  - Treating burnout as an individual problem
- **watch.** Basic conditions and fairness, where chronic overload has become a safety issue. Usually lifts engagement and psychological safety.

### O4-P2 · Scope and mandate renegotiation

- **addresses.** The demand on the unit exceeds any sustainable capacity, and the fix sits above the unit: its scope or mandate has to change, or the enterprise funds it. The unit cannot decide this alone.
- **signs.**
  - The mandate has grown without resourcing
  - The unit absorbs work that belongs to other functions
  - Internal rebalancing has been tried and the gap remains
- **outline.**
  1. Quantify the gap between what the unit is accountable for and what it can sustain.
  2. Build the case in the sponsor's terms: service levels at risk, the cost of turnover and quality.
  3. Set out the options: de-scope with a destination for the work, re-mandate it to the right function, or fund the mandate.
  4. Equip the sponsor to decide; the unit presents, the enterprise decides.
- **forms.** Re-mandate: a formal scope decision at sponsor level. De-scope: move the work to the right function. Resource the mandate: an enterprise funding case.
- **movement.** +5 to +12 if the renegotiation succeeds. The effect depends on a decision outside the unit.
- **projection.** Yes, stated as conditional.
- **pace.** Decision-gated.
- **could_show.** After the decision, then the next half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** B. Role-overload and organisational boundary research.
- **wrong.**
  - Treating it as a unit-level fix; the unit cannot renegotiate its own mandate
  - A de-scope with no agreed destination; the work comes back
  - Winning resource without managing the demand that caused the overload
- **watch.** Basic conditions, where the overload has become unsafe; that raises urgency. Usually lifts engagement and psychological safety once resolved.

### O5-P1 · Targeted manager development

- **addresses.** One manager's enabling behaviour holds their team back. The unit average may be fine while that team sits well below.
- **signs.**
  - Technically excellent, weak at enabling: micromanagement, conflict avoidance, slow decisions
  - Promoted without development
  - Incentives that reward blocking, such as tight cost control over enablement
  - Overloaded, so enabling is what gets dropped
- **outline.**
  1. Diagnose from direct-report perception, peer and own-manager feedback, and observation.
  2. Set specific enabling goals: clear direction, blockers removed, coaching, recognition, fair allocation.
  3. Coach on the manager's real decisions and interactions, not abstract courses.
  4. The manager's own manager reinforces and aligns incentives.
- **forms.** Coaching-led: receptive and changeable. Span reduction: overloaded; coaching with role redesign. Incentive realignment: incentives reward the wrong thing. Role change: development not viable; managed with dignity.
- **movement.** +5 to +10 in the affected team.
- **projection.** No; the range applies to affected teams.
- **pace.** Fast. One of the most responsive sub-dimensions.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read; the quarterly pulse may show it first.
- **evidence.** A for leader effects on teams; B for development effectiveness.
- **wrong.**
  - Generic leadership training instead of targeted change
  - Putting off the role-change decision after six months without movement
  - Coaching a manager whose incentives reward the wrong behaviour
- **watch.** Fairness, where one manager gets development and another with the same shortfall gets nothing. Usually lifts engagement, safety and conflict health in the team.

## Synergy

### S1-P1 · Team composition redesign

- **addresses.** The team's skill profile is the wrong shape: duplicated where overlap adds nothing, thin where range is needed.
- **signs.**
  - Hiring reproduced the existing profile
  - A merge or split ignored the skill mix
  - The work changed and the composition did not
  - Long tenure narrowed everyone into the same specialism
- **outline.**
  1. Map the team's real skill profile against the work.
  2. Find the duplication and the gaps, and the profile that closes them.
  3. Recompose through hiring, internal moves, role redefinition or partner access.
  4. Onboard new members into their complementary role, and brief the team on the new shape.
  5. Review composition against the work each year.
- **forms.** Hiring-led: the gap is fillable by recruitment. Internal mobility: the right people exist elsewhere. Role redefinition: redefining roles closes the gap.
- **movement.** +4 to +10.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Slow. Twelve months or more.
- **could_show.** Annual read.
- **evidence.** B. Team composition and team design research.
- **wrong.**
  - Hiring more of the same
  - Ignoring the effect on cohesion; design the onboarding and norming
  - Adding divergent skill into a team that suppresses it
- **watch.** Fairness, where recomposition looks like targeted exits without due process. Usually lifts collective intelligence.

### S2-P1 · Ways-of-working redesign

- **addresses.** The calendar is consumed by low-value meetings and coordination, leaving too little focus time.
- **signs.**
  - More than 20 meeting hours a week for individual contributors
  - Fragmented days; after-hours catch-up
  - Status updates done synchronously
  - Recurring meetings that outlived their purpose
- **outline.**
  1. Audit meeting load and sort it: necessary recurring, unnecessary recurring, project-bound, ad hoc.
  2. Cut the unnecessary; shorten defaults; trim attendance; move status to written updates.
  3. Protect half-day focus blocks two or three times a week, defended by the manager.
  4. Make written-first the default for decisions; meet only where it adds something.
  5. Redesign the worst recurring handoff.
- **forms.** Meeting reduction: load is the main cost. Focus protection: time is fragmented for other reasons too. Written-first shift: the tools allow it, the practice does not. Handoff: the cost sits in specific handoffs.
- **movement.** +6 to +14.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Fast. Meeting load can change in a fortnight.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read; the quarterly pulse may show it first.
- **evidence.** B. Collaboration overload and interruption research.
- **wrong.**
  - Cutting meetings without redesigning the coordination they did
  - Reverting within two quarters
  - Senior leaders still calling everyone-in meetings while the unit cuts its own
- **watch.** Fairness, in reverse, if fewer meetings reads as exclusion from decisions; set attendance criteria. Usually lifts engagement.

### S3-P1 · Conflict-health practices

- **addresses.** Too little disagreement about the work, or too much friction between people. The two need different fixes and the measurement tells them apart.
- **signs.**
  - Surface agreement, with disagreement re-emerging as passive resistance
  - Interpersonal friction consuming the team's energy
  - Both at once: substance suppressed, venting on people
- **outline.**
  1. Where task conflict is low: norms that invite dissent, an assigned sceptic, pre-mortems; address the safety condition beneath it; reward the dissenter.
  2. Where relationship conflict is high: locate it. A pair gets structured mediation, with role separation if reconciliation fails. A whole team gets a norm reset, sometimes facilitated.
  3. Fix the structural cause where one exists.
- **forms.** Low task conflict: norms, safety work and dissent recognition. Dyadic conflict: mediation, and separation if needed. Team-wide pattern: facilitated reset, norms and the structural fix.
- **movement.** +4 to +9.
- **projection.** Yes.
- **pace.** Moderate. A pair can resolve within a quarter.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** A. Task versus relationship conflict research.
- **wrong.**
  - Inviting dissent while subtle punishment continues
  - Mediation without the authority to enforce the outcome
  - Treating a structural conflict as a personality problem
- **watch.** Fairness, where structural unfairness is the cause. Strongly linked to psychological safety; often one intervention serves both.

## Trip-wire remediations

Trip-wire cards appear above every score and cannot be dismissed at unit level. Each opens with its first action from the Measurement Reference, then the pattern.

### TW-P1 · Pay equity remediation

- **addresses.** The pay equity trip-wire has fired. Pay gaps that role, performance and tenure do not explain, or pay set so opaquely that perception runs ahead of reality.
- **signs.**
  - New hires paid above incumbents in the same band
  - Bands administered inconsistently
  - Gaps between groups with no explanation in role or tenure
  - Nobody can say how pay is set
- **outline.**
  1. A pay equity audit to find the unexplained gaps. Unexplained gaps may carry legal risk; the findings need review before they are acted on.
  2. Remediate them, often over more than one year to avoid a budget shock.
  3. Change the process: calibrated pay decisions, transparent bands and criteria, ongoing monitoring.
  4. Communicate how pay is set, even where individual pay stays private.
- **forms.** Not applicable; the shape follows the audit.
- **movement.** Recovery above 60. Related items lift as the correction becomes visible and trusted.
- **projection.** No; trip-wires sit outside the index.
- **pace.** Slow. Staged correction runs 6 to 24 months; perception moves sooner once trusted.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read for perception; the trip-wire itself at the next full measurement.
- **evidence.** A for the construct; B for remediation effectiveness.
- **wrong.**
  - Partial remediation read as cosmetic, which produces backlash
  - An audit with no follow-through
- **watch.** Lifts engagement and psychological safety once visible and trusted.

### TW-P2 · Fairness remediation

- **addresses.** The fairness trip-wire has fired. A manager or team pattern of unfair treatment, unfair processes in performance management, opportunity or recognition, or informal allocation that disadvantages some groups.
- **signs.**
  - Promotion or opportunity decided by manager discretion with no criteria
  - One team where the pattern concentrates
  - Recognition or allocation that reliably favours the same people
- **outline.**
  1. A fairness investigation, usually led by HR, to find the specific pattern. What it finds may carry legal risk; review the findings before acting on them.
  2. Redesign the unfair process, or act on the manager where a manager is the cause.
  3. Formalise allocation criteria where informal allocation produced the outcome.
  4. Communicate the issue, the response and the ongoing accountability.
- **forms.** Not applicable; the shape follows the investigation.
- **movement.** Recovery above 60; psychological safety and engagement lift with it.
- **projection.** No; trip-wires sit outside the index.
- **pace.** Moderate. Structural fixes in 3 to 12 months; perception follows visible action.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read.
- **evidence.** A for the construct; B for remediation effectiveness.
- **wrong.**
  - Remediation read as targeting a group, which fires fairness from the other side
  - Investigation without structural change
- **watch.** Moves with psychological safety, engagement and conflict health.

### TW-P3 · Basic conditions remediation

- **addresses.** The basic conditions trip-wire has fired. Workspace, equipment, work time or safety is below standard.
- **signs.**
  - Unsafe, cramped or ergonomically poor workspace
  - Tools and equipment chronically unreliable
  - Work-time patterns that cannot be sustained
  - Safety issues raised and not addressed
- **outline.**
  1. A conditions audit: what specifically is below standard, in workspace, tools, time and safety.
  2. Remediate each category with its structural fix.
  3. Install ongoing monitoring: cycled conditions audits and a reviewed safety process.
- **forms.** Not applicable; the shape follows the audit.
- **movement.** Recovery above 60; incident rates fall; engagement and retention lift after.
- **projection.** No; trip-wires sit outside the index.
- **pace.** Variable. Process changes are fast; workspace redesign can take 3 to 24 months.
- **could_show.** Half-yearly read for process fixes; annual for workspace.
- **evidence.** A for the construct; B for intervention effectiveness.
- **wrong.**
  - Fixing the equipment while ignoring the work-time condition that also fired the trip-wire
- **watch.** Moves with engagement, psychological safety and resource adequacy where time is the condition.
