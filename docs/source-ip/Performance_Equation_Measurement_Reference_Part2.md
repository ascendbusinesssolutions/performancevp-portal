# Part 4 — Opportunity (O) Measurement

Opportunity is the degree to which the organisational system enables the unit's people to do their best work. It is measured through five sub-dimensions:

| Sub-dim | Construct | Weight in O |
|---|---|---|
| **O1** | Clarity and decision rights | 30% |
| **O2** | Tools and information | 25% |
| **O3** | Process and workflow | 20% |
| **O4** | Resource adequacy | 10% |
| **O5** | Leadership enablement | 15% |

**Opportunity composite formula:**

```
O = 0.30(O1) + 0.25(O2) + 0.20(O3) + 0.10(O4) + 0.15(O5)
```

Weights sum to 100%. Industry archetype adjustments are documented per sub-dimension in this Measurement Reference (and summarised in the Sub-Dimension × Cadence Master Reference). Note that the *component-level* weights in the equation (α=0.35 Capability, β=0.40 Motivation, γ=0.25 Opportunity) are standardised and held constant across all engagements; industry differences are handled inside the components via these sub-dimension weights, not at the component level.

> **Source of truth.** The sub-dimension structure, the within-component weights and the refresh cadence applied here are owned by the Sub-Dimension × Cadence Master Reference. Where this document repeats those values it does so to be self-contained; if any of them need to change, change them in the Cadence Master first and then propagate.

**Important structural note for O.** O1, O2 and O3 use a paired-measurement design: each combines a **structural audit** (consultant-led, examining what exists on paper) with a **perception survey** (employee-completed, examining what is experienced). Where the two agree (gap of 15 points or less), they are averaged into the sub-dimension score. Where they diverge by more than 15 points (the audit-perception gap rule, detailed in Part 8), both are reported separately as a key diagnostic finding and the perception score is the value that carries into the composite. This paired design is one of the distinguishing methodological features of the Performance Equation.

## 4.1 O1 — Clarity and decision rights

### Construct

**Definition.** The combined coverage of decision-rights distribution (who is authorised to decide what), role clarity (what each person is expected to do and accountable for), and strategic-alignment cascade (how individual and team work connects to enterprise priorities). O1 consolidates the previously separate decision rights, role clarity and strategic clarity sub-dimensions on the basis that respondents experience these as one question — "do I know what I am meant to do, what I am authorised to decide, and how my work connects up".

**Plain language.** Whether people in this unit know what they are meant to do, what they have authority to decide, and how their work connects to the broader organisation.

**Theoretical foundation.** Role theory (Katz and Kahn, 1978; Rizzo et al., 1970 on role ambiguity and conflict). Decision rights frameworks (RAPID, RACI, DACI). Organisation design literature (Galbraith Star Model). Strategy execution research (Kaplan and Norton on cascade; Ulrich on line-of-sight). Cumulative evidence: Tubre & Collins (2000) and Gilboa et al. (2008), meta-analyses, that role ambiguity carries a consistent negative relationship with performance; Locke & Latham (2002) on specific, connected goals outperforming vague ones across hundreds of studies (the warrant for auditing the strategic cascade); Rogers & Blenko (2006) and Blenko et al. (2010, practitioner-grade), Bain's decision-effectiveness programme linking clear decision roles to financial performance. Within the Opportunity set, clarity constructs carry the strongest cumulative evidence, supporting the 30% weight; role theory locates the damage in experienced ambiguity, supporting the perception score carrying the composite when the audit-perception gap fires.

### Weight

Default 30% of O. Industry archetypes:

| Archetype | O1 weight |
|---|---|
| Default | 30% |
| Knowledge-intensive | 30% |
| Operations-heavy | 25% (process clarity O3 carries more weight) |
| Customer-facing service | 35% (frontline decision authority critical) |
| Public sector / regulated | 30% |
| Healthcare | 30% |

### Tier 3 — OI-Clarity audit and survey

The OI-Clarity audit and survey is the PerformanceVP Tier 3 instrument for O1.

**Audit component (consultant-led, half-day workshop):**

1. **Decision rights mapping.** The consultant works with the unit's leadership team to map the unit's 8–12 most consequential recurring decision types against a decision-rights framework (RAPID applied per decision: who Recommends, Agrees, Performs, has Input, Decides). Score each decision on completeness and clarity (0–10).
2. **Role architecture review.** The consultant reviews position descriptions and accountability documentation for the unit's role families. Score on coverage and currency (0–10).
3. **Strategic cascade audit.** The consultant traces 5–10 goal-traces from enterprise priorities to unit-level goals to team and individual goals. Score on coherence and completeness (0–10).

**Audit score calculation:**

```
Decision rights audit component = (mean across decisions, scaled to 0–100)
Role architecture audit component = (score × 10)
Strategic cascade audit component = (mean across goal-traces, scaled to 0–100)

O1 audit score = 0.40 × Decision rights + 0.30 × Role architecture + 0.30 × Strategic cascade
```

**Perception component (8 items in the Performance Equation Diagnostic Survey):**

The OI1 module is 8 items in the Survey Blueprint Part 4.1 covering role clarity, decision rights and strategic cascade dimensions.

**Perception score calculation:**

```
O1 perception score = ((mean of valid OI1 items, OI1-03 and OI1-06 reverse-scored) − 1) × 25
```

### Operational conversion rule

The consultant records inputs in the workbook's "O1 Clarity and Decision Rights" section:

**Audit input:**

| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| Decision rights audit score | 0–100, from the audit per the scoring above |
| Role architecture audit score | 0–100 |
| Strategic cascade audit score | 0–100 |
| Composite audit score | Calculated per the weighting above |
| Number of decisions audited | Audit sample size |
| Number of goal-traces audited | |
| Audit date | |

**Perception input:**

| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| Source identifier | Platform / module |
| Source vintage | |
| Response rate | |
| O1 perception score | 0–100, from OI1 items |

**Sub-dimension score with gap rule:**

```
Gap = |O1 audit score - O1 perception score|

If Gap ≤ 15:
  O1 = (O1 audit score + O1 perception score) / 2

If Gap > 15:
  O1 = O1 perception score   [the value that feeds the O composite]
  Diagnostic finding: "Audit-perception gap of [Gap] points" flagged in report
  Both scores shown separately in the report's O1 section
```

When the gap flag fires, the perception score carries into the composite (aligned with Diagnostic Workbook Spec 6.6); both layers and the gap magnitude are reported prominently, and the diagnostic finding becomes part of the narrative.

### Worked example

A Customer Service unit of 60 FTE. Tier 3 OI-Clarity audit deployed + OI1 perception survey.

**Audit:**
- 10 decisions audited: 4 fully mapped with clear RAPID assignment (score 9–10), 4 partially mapped (score 5–7), 2 unclear (score 2–4). Mean = 6.2. Scaled to 0–100: 62.
- Role architecture: position descriptions current for 7 of 8 role families, success criteria documented for 4. Score 6. Scaled: 60.
- Strategic cascade: 8 goal-traces audited; 5 show clear cascade, 2 show partial alignment, 1 shows disconnect. Mean 6.5. Scaled: 65.

O1 audit score = 0.40 × 62 + 0.30 × 60 + 0.30 × 65 = 24.8 + 18.0 + 19.5 = 62.3

**Perception:**
- OI1 module deployed, 48 valid respondents (80% response rate)
- Mean across 8 items (after reverse-scoring): 3.4
- O1 perception score = (3.4 - 1) × 25 = 60.0

**Gap analysis:**
- Gap = |62.3 - 60.0| = 2.3 → no gap flag.
- O1 = (62.3 + 60.0) / 2 = 61.2

**Unit O1 = 61.2, Tier 3 audit + Tier 3 perception survey, Confidence: High.**

Interpretation: structural clarity and experienced clarity are aligned at a moderate level. The diagnostic narrative would identify decision rights mapping (the lowest audit component) as the lever for improvement.

A contrasting example where the gap rule fires:

Same unit, alternative scenario:
- Audit score: 78 (decision rights well-mapped, position descriptions current)
- Perception score: 56 (employees experience role confusion)
- Gap = 22 → gap flag fires
- O1 = 56 (the perception score feeds the composite when the gap flag fires)
- Finding: "Audit-perception gap of 22 points. Structural decision-rights framework exists on paper at a high standard but is not experienced as working by the unit's people. Likely causes include change in leadership behaviour, recent restructure not yet absorbed, or framework not communicated effectively. Recommended action: clarity recommunication workshop, manager coaching."

That divergence pattern is more diagnostically valuable than a single number.

### Reporting threshold and confidence

O1 requires both the audit and perception components to be measured. Perception alone with no audit = Tier 2 at best; audit alone with no perception score = insufficient for full O1 score.

Confidence:
- Audit: High for 12 months, Medium at 14, Low at 18
- Perception: High for 6 months, Medium at 8, Low at 12
- Composite O1: takes the more restrictive of the two

## 4.2 O2 — Tools and information

### Construct

**Definition.** Fit-for-purpose technology, systems and data access. Whether unit members have the tools they need to do their work, can access the information required, and experience their tool stack as enabling rather than obstructing.

**Plain language.** Whether the technology, systems and data this unit needs to do its work are actually fit for purpose.

**Theoretical foundation.** Sociotechnical systems theory (Trist and Bamforth, 1951; Emery; later Pasmore) — performance is jointly produced by the social and technical systems, and a broken technical system caps what capable, motivated people can produce (a micro-foundation for the multiplicative logic). Information systems success theory (DeLone and McLean, 1992, 2003), with Petter, DeLone & McLean (2008) reviewing the evidence: system quality and information quality drive use and net benefits — the O2 audit's three components (tool inventory, information access, integration) map onto this model directly. Adjacent: digital workplace and employee experience research.

### Weight

Default 25% of O. Industry archetypes:

| Archetype | O2 weight |
|---|---|
| Default | 25% |
| Knowledge-intensive | 30% (tools and information central to expert work) |
| Operations-heavy | 25% |
| Customer-facing service | 25% |
| Public sector / regulated | 20% |
| Healthcare | 30% (clinical systems critical) |

### Tier 1 sources

**Tier 1 acceptance criteria (structural):** All three of the following:

1. SaaS management platform (e.g. Zylo, Productiv, Torii) providing tool inventory and adoption telemetry.
2. ITSM platform (ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, Freshservice) with ticket data on tool-related issues affecting the unit.
3. Workplace Analytics or equivalent platform (Microsoft Viva Insights, Worklytics) providing tool-usage telemetry per the unit.

**Tier 1 acceptance criteria (perception):** Engagement survey with construct-aligned tools-and-information sub-scale, alpha ≥ 0.75, response rate ≥ 60%.

### Tier 2 sources

For the structural component:

| Source | Conversion rule |
|---|---|
| Partial tool inventory (e.g. asset management system) | Use where ≥ 80% of the unit's tools are documented |
| ITSM data alone | Use ticket volume and resolution time as triangulator; less rigorous than full structural audit |
| IT spend allocation per FTE | Comparative metric only |
| Recent IT satisfaction surveys | Where construct-aligned |

For the perception component: Single-item tools satisfaction in engagement survey; internal pulse surveys with tools items.

### Tier 3 — OI-2 audit and survey

**Audit component (consultant-led, half-day with IT operations):**

1. **Tool inventory.** Document the unit's tool stack — primary systems, supporting tools, data sources. Score on completeness, integration quality, fitness for purpose (0–10 each).
2. **Information access audit.** Trace 5–8 typical information needs from the unit (e.g. "frontline employee accessing customer history", "manager accessing performance data", "analyst accessing operational metrics"). Score on accessibility, timeliness, accuracy (0–10).
3. **Tool integration assessment.** Document integration vs. fragmentation between primary systems. Score on workflow continuity (0–10).

**Audit score calculation:**

```
Tool inventory score = mean(completeness, integration quality, fitness for purpose) × 10
Information access score = (mean across information traces, scaled to 0–100)
Integration score = score × 10

O2 audit score = 0.35 × Tool inventory + 0.40 × Information access + 0.25 × Integration
```

**Perception component (4 items in the Performance Equation Diagnostic Survey, OI2 module).**

```
O2 perception score = ((mean of valid OI2 items, OI2-04 reverse-scored) − 1) × 25
```

**Perception input:**

| Input | Description |

| Source vintage | |
| Response rate | |
| O2 perception score | 0–100 |

**Sub-dimension score with gap rule:**

```
Gap = |O2 audit score - O2 perception score|

If Gap ≤ 15: O2 = (O2 audit score + O2 perception score) / 2
If Gap > 15: O2 = O2 perception score; gap flag, both shown separately in report
```

### Worked example

A Professional Services unit of 100 FTE.

**Audit:**
- Tool inventory: complete, well-integrated CRM + practice management + document management; integration score 8, completeness 9, fitness 7. Mean 8.0. Scaled: 80.
- Information access: 6 information traces audited; 4 work well, 2 require workarounds. Mean 7.0. Scaled: 70.
- Integration: 3 primary systems with API-level integration but some manual handoffs. Score 7. Scaled: 70.

O2 audit score = 0.35 × 80 + 0.40 × 70 + 0.25 × 70 = 28.0 + 28.0 + 17.5 = 73.5

- OI2 module deployed, 82 valid respondents (82% response rate)
- Mean across 4 items (after reverse-scoring): 3.6
- O2 perception score = (3.6 - 1) × 25 = 65.0

**Gap analysis:**
- Gap = |73.5 - 65.0| = 8.5 → no gap flag
- O2 = (73.5 + 65.0) / 2 = 69.3

**Unit O2 = 69.3, Tier 3 audit + Tier 3 perception, Confidence: High.**

### Reporting threshold and confidence

Both audit and perception components required.

Confidence:
- Audit: High for 12 months, Medium at 14, Low at 18
- Perception: High for 6 months, Medium at 8, Low at 12

## 4.3 O3 — Process and workflow

### Construct

**Definition.** Friction-free flow of work through the unit. Cycle times, rework, value-adding versus non-value-adding steps, handoff quality.

**Plain language.** Whether the way work flows through this unit is well-designed — work moves efficiently, doesn't get stuck unnecessarily, and people aren't doing duplicate effort or rework.

**Theoretical foundation.** Lean process design (Womack, Jones and Roos, 1990; Womack and Jones, 1996), whose IMVP programme documented large, persistent plant-level performance differentials attributable to process design; Shah & Ward (2003), large-sample evidence that lean practice bundles relate to operational performance. Business process management (Hammer and Champy, Davenport). Theory of Constraints (Goldratt) for bottleneck analysis — the intellectual cousin of the Diagnostic's binding-constraint method (throughput is governed by the constraint; improving elsewhere is largely wasted). Process mining literature (van der Aalst, 2016) for empirical methodology. The operations-heavy uplift to 30% follows the evidence being strongest where work is process-bound.

### Weight

Default 20% of O. Industry archetypes:

| Archetype | O3 weight |
|---|---|
| Default | 20% |
| Knowledge-intensive | 15% (knowledge work less process-bound) |
| Operations-heavy | 30% (process is the core of the work) |
| Customer-facing service | 20% |
| Public sector / regulated | 25% (process compliance and efficiency both matter) |
| Healthcare | 25% (clinical pathways highly process-dependent) |

### Tier 1 sources

**Tier 1 acceptance criteria (structural):** Process mining platform with coverage of the unit's core processes, vintage within 12 months, providing cycle time, rework rate and value-stream visualisation.

|---|---|

3. **Aggregate audit score = mean across audited processes, scaled to 0–100.**

The audit runs approximately 1 consulting day per process audited.

**Perception component (5 items in OI3 module).**

```

### Worked example

A Claims Processing unit of 150 FTE.

Both audit and perception components required, with at least 3 processes audited.

Confidence:
- Audit: High for 12 months, Medium at 14, Low at 18

## 4.4 O4 — Resource adequacy

### Construct

**Definition.** Whether the unit has time, headcount and budget proportionate to demand. Workload-capacity fit.

**Plain language.** Whether this unit has enough people, time and budget to do what's being asked of it.

**Theoretical foundation.** Job Demands-Resources model (Bakker and Demerouti, 2007, 2017) — resource adequacy as a key determinant of sustainable performance. Cumulative evidence: Crawford, LePine & Rich (2010), meta-analytic test confirming that hindrance demands (including chronic overload) predict burnout while resources predict engagement; Lee & Ashforth (1996) and Maslach et al. (2001) on workload as a core, replicated driver of exhaustion. Workforce planning and capacity management theory. The JD-R construct is inherently a demands-versus-resources experience, which is why O4 blends objective capacity analysis with perception without a gap rule; the modest 10% weight avoids double-counting effect that flows through the motivation pathway.

### Weight

Default 10% of O. Industry archetypes:

| Archetype | O4 weight |
|---|---|
| Default | 10% |
| Knowledge-intensive | 10% |
| Operations-heavy | 15% (capacity directly affects throughput) |
| Customer-facing service | 15% |
| Public sector / regulated | 10% |
| Healthcare | 15% (resourcing affects patient outcomes) |

### Tier 1 sources

**Tier 1 acceptance criteria:** Workforce planning data combined with capacity analytics and HRIS overtime/after-hours data, providing quantified demand-versus-capacity visibility.

Acceptable platforms:

| Platform | Notes |
|---|---|
| Visier | Workforce planning and analytics |
| Anaplan | Where workforce planning module is deployed |
| Workday Adaptive Planning | |
| HRIS overtime / after-hours data | Behavioural triangulator |
| Internal capacity-modelling tools | Where documented methodology |

### Tier 2 sources

| Source | Conversion rule |
|---|---|
| Project capacity data (from work management systems) | Where utilisation is tracked |
| Backlog data | Backlog growth rate as inverse capacity signal |
| Burnout indicators | Sick leave clustering, absenteeism patterns; behavioural triangulator |
| Engagement-survey workload items | Where construct-aligned |

### Tier 3 — OI-4 survey and capacity analysis

**Capacity analysis component (consultant-led):**

The consultant works with the unit's leadership and HRIS data to assess capacity:

1. **Workload vs. capacity.** Compare unit demand (volumes, output expectations, project commitments) against capacity (FTE × productive hours per period × historical productivity).
2. **Behavioural capacity stress signals.** Overtime rate, after-hours collaboration volume, sick leave clustering.
3. **Backlog and waiting work.** Volume and age of work waiting to be done.

**Capacity analysis score:**

```
Capacity analysis score = composite based on:
  - Workload-to-capacity ratio (target 80–95% utilisation; over 105% or under 70% = lower score)
  - Behavioural stress indicators (each on 0–10 inverse scale)
  - Backlog stability (stable backlog = higher score; growing backlog = lower)

Scaled to 0–100
```

**Perception component (3 items in OI4 module).**

```
O4 perception score = ((mean of valid OI4 items, OI4-03 reverse-scored) − 1) × 25
```

### Operational conversion rule

The consultant records inputs in the workbook's "O4 Resource Adequacy" section:

**Capacity analysis input:**

| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| Workload-to-capacity ratio | % (e.g. 110% = 10% over capacity) |
| Overtime rate | Hours per FTE per period above standard |
| Sick leave rate | Compared to baseline (% above/below) |
| Backlog age | Median days in backlog |
| Capacity analysis score | 0–100, computed from above |
| Analysis date | |

**Perception input:**

| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| Source identifier | |
| Source vintage | |
| Response rate | |
| O4 perception score | 0–100 |

**Sub-dimension score:**

For O4, unlike O1/O2/O3, the structural component (capacity analysis) and perception component are blended directly (no gap rule applied) because the construct is intrinsically about both objective capacity and experienced workload — divergence is interpretable as ordinary measurement variance, not as a gap finding.

```
O4 = (O4 capacity analysis score + O4 perception score) / 2
```

### Worked example

An Engineering unit of 80 FTE.

**Capacity analysis:**
- Workload-to-capacity ratio: 108% (slight overload)
- Overtime rate: 4.5 hours per FTE per week above standard (industry norm 2.0)
- Sick leave: 12% above baseline
- Backlog age: median 18 days (target 10)

Capacity analysis composite: stress signals indicate moderate-high pressure. Score 55.

**Perception:**
- OI4 module deployed, 64 valid respondents (80%)
- Mean across 3 items (after reverse-scoring): 2.6
- O4 perception score = (2.6 - 1) × 25 = 40.0

**Sub-dimension score:**
O4 = (55 + 40) / 2 = 47.5

**Unit O4 = 47.5, Tier 3 capacity analysis + Tier 3 perception, Confidence: High.**

Interpretation: clear capacity strain in this unit, corroborated by both objective stress signals and perception data. The diagnostic narrative would surface O4 as a contributor to overall P score weakness and recommend capacity intervention.

### Reporting threshold and confidence

Both components required.

Confidence:
- Capacity analysis: High for 6 months, Medium at 8, Low at 12 (underlying data feeds monthly via HRIS, but the analytical composite is half-yearly)
- Perception: High for 6 months, Medium at 8, Low at 12

## 4.5 O5 — Leadership enablement

### Construct

**Definition.** Degree to which managers unblock rather than block their teams. Direct-report perception of manager enabling behaviours — removing obstacles, backing decisions, making work easier. Consolidates the leadership-effectiveness aspect of the original O7 with the leadership-behaviour component of the original C6, on the basis that leadership belongs in the system that enables performance (O) rather than in the capability of the people being led (C).

**Plain language.** Whether managers in this unit are actively helping their teams perform — clearing obstacles, supporting decisions — or getting in the way.

**Theoretical foundation.** Servant leadership (Greenleaf; Liden et al., 2008 for measurement). Empowering leadership research (Arnold et al., 2000; Cheong et al.). Adjacent: leader-member exchange theory (Graen and Uhl-Bien); transformational leadership (Bass) where the enabling aspect is relevant. Cumulative evidence: Lee, Willis & Tian (2018), meta-analysis of 105 samples, that empowering leadership predicts performance, citizenship and creativity at individual and team level with incremental validity beyond transformational leadership and LMX, team effects mediated by empowerment; Hoch et al. (2018) on servant leadership's incremental validity; Gerstner & Day (1997), LMX meta-analysis. The meta-analytic effects run through empowerment and obstacle-removal — the system around the team — which is why leadership sits in Opportunity rather than Capability.

### Weight

## 4.6 Opportunity composite scoring

The Opportunity composite is the weighted sum of the five sub-dimensions:

# Part 5 — Synergy (S) Measurement

Synergy is the team-level interaction quality that amplifies or drags on the underlying Capability, Motivation and Opportunity composites. It is measured through three sub-dimensions:

| Sub-dim | Construct | Weight in S_internal |
|---|---|---|
| **S1** | Skill complementarity | 30% |
| **S2** | Collaboration friction | 40% |
| **S3** | Conflict health | 30% |

**Synergy coefficient formula:**

```
S_internal = 0.30(S1) + 0.40(S2) + 0.30(S3)

S = 0.85 + (S_internal / 100) × 0.30
```

This maps S_internal ∈ [0, 100] to S ∈ [0.85, 1.15]. A neutral S_internal of 50 produces S = 1.00. The asymmetric mapping — neutral synergy is at S_internal = 50, not 0 — reflects that Synergy is an amplification/drag factor on positive baseline performance, not a measure of presence-versus-absence.

## 5.1 S1 — Skill complementarity

### Construct

**Definition.** Diversity and complementarity of capabilities across the unit's members. Whether the unit's skill profile is genuinely complementary (different members bring different strengths covering the needed range) or overlapping (members duplicate each other without covering needed range).

**Plain language.** Whether this unit's people bring genuinely different and complementary strengths, rather than a lot of the same skill stacked up without coverage of what's actually needed.

**Theoretical foundation.** Team composition research (Mathieu et al., 2014); team diversity literature (van Knippenberg and Schippers, 2007; Stahl et al. on cultural diversity). Optimal team-composition theory (Hackman; Wageman). Cumulative evidence: Bell (2007), meta-analysis, that deep-level composition variables predict team performance (who is combined with whom matters beyond individual quality); Humphrey, Morgeson & Mannor (2009) on the strategic core (capability concentrated in core roles matters disproportionately) — the warrant for the depth-coverage component. The information/decision-making view of diversity supports measuring task-relevant complementarity rather than demographic diversity.

### Weight

Default 30% of S_internal. Industry archetypes:

| Archetype | S1 weight |
|---|---|
| Default | 30% |
| Knowledge-intensive | 35% (complementarity central to expert team output) |
| Operations-heavy | 25% (more standardised roles; complementarity less variable) |
| Customer-facing service | 30% |
| Public sector / regulated | 30% |
| Healthcare | 30% (multidisciplinary teams) |

### Tier 1 sources

**Tier 1 acceptance criteria:** Skills inventory data per team member with manager-confirmed proficiency (the same source data that feeds C1), at unit-level coverage of ≥ 75%.

Acceptable platforms: as for C1 (Workday Skills Cloud, SuccessFactors, Oracle, Korn Ferry Success Profiles).

S1 uses the C1 source data analytically — it does not require new data collection. The same skill records that feed C1 are analysed for complementarity patterns.

### Tier 2 sources

| Source | Conversion rule |
|---|---|
| Capability framework with required skills per role + manager proficiency ratings | Where C1 Tier 2 source exists |
| HRIS data with role profiles + tenure | Less precise; downgrade |
| Industry standard role-skill maps | Where applied to the unit |

### Tier 3 — TSI-1 Skill Complementarity Analysis

The PerformanceVP TSI-1 is an analytical protocol applied to existing data plus, where needed, a structured workshop:

1. **Existing data analysis** (where any skills inventory exists): The consultant analyses the team-level skill profile per the rules below.
2. **Workshop component** (where no skills data exists): A 2-hour workshop with the unit's leadership identifies the unit's 8–12 critical capability needs and assesses coverage across team members.

The TSI-1 analysis runs approximately 1 consulting day per unit.

### Operational conversion rule

The consultant records inputs in the workbook's "S1 Skill Complementarity" section. The analytical approach computes three components:

**1. Coverage breadth.** What proportion of the unit's required capability spectrum is covered by at least one proficient team member.

```
Coverage breadth = (# distinct required skills covered by ≥1 proficient member)
                  / (total # distinct required skills)
                × 100
```

**2. Coverage depth.** How many team members can perform each critical capability — too few creates single points of failure; too many indicates overlap without complementarity.

```
For each critical capability:
  Depth coverage = min(# proficient members / target depth, 1.0)

where target depth typically = ceiling(team_FTE / 10) per critical capability,
with minimum of 2.

Depth score = (mean of depth coverage across critical capabilities) × 100
```

**3. Distribution profile.** Whether the unit's skills are distributed across members (high complementarity) or stacked on a few members (low complementarity).

```
Distribution score = 100 - Gini coefficient of skill-count-per-member × 100

(High Gini = unequal distribution = low score)
```

**S1 composite:**

```
S1 = 0.40 × Coverage breadth + 0.35 × Depth score + 0.25 × Distribution score
```

### Worked example

A Software Engineering unit of 30 FTE with skills data from Workday (Tier 1).

The unit operates against a 15-skill framework: 8 critical skills (e.g. Python, React, AWS, system design) and 7 supporting skills.

**Coverage breadth:** 14 of 15 framework skills have at least one proficient member. Breadth = 14/15 × 100 = 93.3.

**Coverage depth:** Target depth = ceiling(30/10) = 3 per critical skill. Audit shows: 7 of 8 critical skills meet target depth; 1 (system design) has only 1 proficient member. Mean depth coverage = (7 + 1/3) / 8 = 0.917. Depth score = 91.7.

**Distribution:** Skill counts per member range from 4 to 11; Gini coefficient = 0.18 (relatively even). Distribution score = 100 - 18 = 82.

**S1 composite:** 0.40 × 93.3 + 0.35 × 91.7 + 0.25 × 82 = 37.3 + 32.1 + 20.5 = 89.9

**Unit S1 = 89.9, Tier 1 Workday Skills Cloud, Confidence: High.**

Interpretation: strong skill complementarity, with one specific risk noted in the methodology footer — system design depth is a single point of failure.

### Reporting threshold and confidence

S1 requires the underlying C1 skills data to be available at the unit level with at least 75% FTE coverage.

Confidence: tied to C1 confidence (inherits from the underlying data).

## 5.2 S2 — Collaboration friction

### Construct

**Definition.** Meeting overload, handoff failures, rework, fragmented time, coordination cost. The operational drag on team output from poor collaboration design.

**Plain language.** Whether the way this unit collaborates — meetings, handoffs, coordination — works smoothly or creates unnecessary drag on the actual work.

**Theoretical foundation.** Steiner (1972) process-loss formalism — actual productivity equals potential productivity minus losses from faulty process; S2 measures the loss term directly. Organisational network analysis (Cross, Borgatti and Parker, 2002). Collaboration overload research (Cross, Rebele and Grant, 2016; Perlow, 1999 on fragmented time). Coordination cost theory (Malone and Crowston, 1994); Mark et al. (2008) on the cost of interruption. Cumulative evidence: LePine et al. (2008) and Marks et al. (2001) — teamwork-process quality relates moderately and consistently to team performance. As the purest operationalisation of the process-loss term and the most telemetry-measurable Synergy construct, S2 carries the heaviest Synergy weight (40%).

### Weight

| Healthcare | 40% |

```

### Worked example

A Professional Services unit of 100 FTE. Microsoft Workplace Analytics deployed (Tier 1).

## 5.3 S3 — Conflict health

### Construct

**Definition.** Ratio of constructive task conflict (productive disagreement on ideas, methods, approaches) to destructive relationship conflict (interpersonal friction, personality clashes, harboured grudges). Drawn from De Dreu and Weingart meta-analytic distinction.

**Plain language.** Whether this unit's people debate work decisions productively without that debate spilling into personal animosity. Healthy conflict — about the work — alongside low interpersonal friction.

**Theoretical foundation.** Jehn (1995, 1997) — the foundational task/relationship/process conflict typology. De Dreu and Weingart (2003) and de Wit, Greer & Jehn (2012, 116 studies, 8,880 groups) — the meta-analytic evidence: relationship conflict is robustly destructive, while task conflict turns positive precisely where it is decoupled from relationship conflict and for decision-quality outcomes. That moderator is the S3 construct: S3 scores the ratio of task to relationship conflict (task items forward-scored, relationship items reverse-scored), not the volume of debate. Adjacent: psychological safety research (Edmondson) for the safety-to-disagree component and the false-consensus flag.

### Weight

Default 30% of S_internal. Industry archetypes:

| Archetype | S3 weight |
|---|---|
| Default | 30% |
| Knowledge-intensive | 35% (productive disagreement central to expert work) |
| Operations-heavy | 25% |
| Customer-facing service | 30% |
| Public sector / regulated | 30% |
| Healthcare | 30% (clinical decision quality benefits from productive disagreement) |

### Tier 1 sources

**There is no Tier 1 source.** No major commercial platform measures task vs. relationship conflict separately. This is genuine PerformanceVP measurement IP territory.

### Tier 2 sources

| Source | Conversion rule |
|---|---|

| Retrospective data flagging conflict events | Themed coding |
| Engagement-survey debate-culture items where present | Where construct-aligned |
| Manager observation of team dynamics | Documented assessments |

### Tier 3 — TSI-3 Conflict Health Survey

The TSI-3 module is 5 items in the Performance Equation Diagnostic Survey. Items in Survey Blueprint Part 5.2. Three items measure task conflict (forward-scored, higher is better) and two measure relationship conflict (reverse-scored).

```
S3 score = ((mean of valid TSI3 items, TSI3-04 and TSI3-05 reverse-scored) − 1) × 25
```

A high S3 score requires both high task conflict (productive debate) AND low relationship conflict (low interpersonal friction). The reverse-scoring of the relationship conflict items achieves this symmetrically.

### Operational conversion rule

The consultant records inputs in the workbook's "S3 Conflict Health" section:

| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| Source identifier | TSI-3 module |
| Source vintage | |
| Response rate | |
| Task conflict items mean | TSI3-01 to TSI3-03 mean |
| Relationship conflict items mean (raw) | TSI3-04 and TSI3-05 mean (before reverse-scoring) |
| Relationship conflict items mean (adjusted) | After reverse-scoring |
| S3 score | 0–100, from all 5 items after reverse-scoring |

**False-consensus pattern flag.** Where the task-conflict items (TSI3-01, TSI3-02) score below 60 AND psychological safety (M2) scores above 75, this is flagged as a "false consensus" pattern — apparent safety masking suppressed disagreement. Reported as a separate diagnostic finding.

### Worked example

A Product Management unit of 40 FTE.

**Survey (TSI-3 module):**
- 32 valid respondents (80% response)
- TSI3-01 (challenge each other's ideas openly): mean 3.8
- TSI3-02 (debate productively): mean 3.6
- TSI3-03 (comfortable with robust discussions): mean 3.4
- TSI3-04 (interpersonal friction — reverse): raw 2.4 → adjusted 3.6
- TSI3-05 (personality clashes — reverse): raw 2.2 → adjusted 3.8

Mean across 5 adjusted items: (3.8 + 3.6 + 3.4 + 3.6 + 3.8) / 5 = 3.64
S3 score = (3.64 - 1) × 25 = 66.0

**Unit S3 = 66.0, Tier 3 TSI-3, Confidence: High.**

Interpretation: moderate conflict health — productive debate happens reasonably (task conflict items at 3.4–3.8) and interpersonal friction is moderate-low (relationship conflict raw scores 2.2–2.4 — below midpoint, which is desirable). The unit could push toward more openly contested debate (TSI3-03 lowest at 3.4) while maintaining the existing low relationship-conflict baseline.

### Reporting threshold and confidence

S3 survey requires ≥ 60% response rate per unit.

Confidence:
- High for 6 months
- Medium at 8 months
- Low at 12 months

## 5.4 Synergy coefficient scoring

The Synergy internal composite is the weighted sum of the three sub-dimensions:

```
S_internal = 0.30(S1) + 0.40(S2) + 0.30(S3)
```

This is mapped to the Synergy coefficient via:

```
S = 0.85 + (S_internal / 100) × 0.30
```

**Mapping table for reference:**

| S_internal | S coefficient | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.85 | Maximum drag (theoretical floor) |
| 25 | 0.925 | Substantial drag |
| 50 | 1.000 | Neutral synergy |
| 75 | 1.075 | Substantial amplification |
| 100 | 1.150 | Maximum amplification (theoretical ceiling) |

In practice, S_internal scores typically fall in the 35–75 range, producing S coefficients between 0.955 and 1.075. The bounded range prevents Synergy from dominating P; a unit with strong fundamentals (high C, M, O) but poor Synergy still produces a reasonable P, just lower than the same unit would achieve with strong Synergy.

**Handling of missing sub-dimensions.** Where a Synergy sub-dimension is unmeasurable, its weight is reallocated proportionally, with the omission documented. Where all three are unmeasurable, S defaults to 1.00 (neutral) with a documented "Synergy not measured" note in the methodology footer.

# Part 6 — Decision Latency Protocol

The Decision Latency Protocol (DLP) is the PerformanceVP flagship objective metric, reported alongside the Opportunity composite. It does not feed into the O composite or the P calculation directly; it stands as an independently-reported diagnostic that often becomes the most actionable finding in a Performance Equation engagement.

## 6.1 Construct

**Definition.** The elapsed time from problem identification (a decision being recognised as needed) to authorised execution (the decision being made and resourced for action), measured across a sample of decisions stratified by decision class.

**Plain language.** How long it actually takes for decisions to get made in this unit, broken down by the type of decision.

**Why it matters.** Decision latency is a leading indicator of organisational performance degradation that often appears before lagging measures (revenue, customer satisfaction) move. Long latency suppresses opportunity capture, frustrates capable people (driving M down), and signals dysfunction in decision rights (which sits in O1). The DLP gives leadership a hard number where most organisations have only anecdotal evidence.

**Evidence foundation.** Eisenhardt (1989), the founding field study: fast decision makers outperform, and achieve speed through more information and more alternatives rather than less rigour. Baum & Wally (2003), a four-year longitudinal study of 318 CEOs, found decision speed predicts subsequent firm growth and profitability — a speed-then-performance design that is the direct warrant for the leading-indicator framing. Rogers & Blenko (2006) and Blenko et al. (2010, practitioner-grade) locate decision effectiveness (quality, speed, yield, effort) in decision-role clarity and governance design, supporting the DLP-to-O1 diagnostic pathway. McKinsey decision-making survey research (De Smet and colleagues, practitioner-grade) documents the cost of slow decisions.

## 6.2 The three decision classes

The DLP samples decisions stratified across three classes:

| Class | Definition | Typical latency norm |
|---|---|---|
| **Operational** | Day-to-day decisions affecting routine work — process exceptions, resource shifts, customer accommodations | 0–7 days |
| **Tactical** | Medium-term decisions affecting how a team or unit operates — new hire approvals, project prioritisation, vendor selection, technology adoption | 7–30 days |
| **Strategic** | Long-term decisions affecting unit direction or capability — major investment, organisational change, strategic priority shift | 30–180 days |

Sampling is stratified to capture all three classes proportional to their occurrence in the unit's decision stream.

## 6.3 Sampling methodology

For a full baseline DLP deployment, the consultant samples 30–60 decisions across the three classes:

| Class | Baseline sample | Half-yearly refresh | Quarterly pulse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operational | 15–25 decisions | 5–10 decisions | 5–8 decisions |
| Tactical | 10–20 decisions | 5–10 decisions | Not refreshed |
| Strategic | 5–15 decisions | Not refreshed | Not refreshed |

**Sampling sources:**

- Decision records and minutes from formal governance forums
- Calendar invitations for decision-relevant meetings, traced to outcomes
- Ticketing and approval systems
- Structured interviews with unit leadership identifying recent decisions

**Sampling rules:**

- Decisions sampled must have a definable "problem identified" date and a definable "authorised execution" date
- Decisions still in flight at the time of sampling are tracked with an "open at sampling" flag (used as a lower-bound latency indicator)
- Sampling should cover the period preceding the engagement, not the engagement period itself (to avoid the engagement perturbing latency measurement)

## 6.4 Data collection per decision

For each sampled decision, the consultant captures:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Decision identifier | Anonymised label |
| Decision class | Operational / Tactical / Strategic |
| Problem identified date | When the decision need was first recognised |
| Decision authorised date | When execution authority was given |
| Latency (days) | Authorised date - identified date |
| Decision stakeholders | Who participated |
| Decision pathway | What approvals were required, in what sequence |
| Bottleneck (if any) | Which approval step caused the longest delay |
| Outcome quality (if assessable) | Whether the decision quality was high, adequate, or poor in hindsight |
| Decision type | Subcategorisation (e.g. hiring, project, process) |

Data is collected through a combination of:

1. Document review of formal decision records
2. The M-DLP-PART decision-participant module (async; the consultant pre-populates decision metadata from documentary evidence and participants confirm or correct), per the Tier 3 Module Library Part 6.1
3. A small number of retained structured interviews (typically 1–3 strategic decisions, or where survey responses leave ambiguity)
4. Calendar / system trace where available

The DLP deployment runs approximately 2–3 consulting days for a full baseline (module-based, plus 30–60 minutes of retained interviews per unit), depending on decision availability and complexity.

## 6.5 Scoring

The Decision Latency Score (DLS) is computed per decision class and overall.

**Per-class score:**

For each decision class, latency observations are compared to class norms:

```
For each decision:
  If latency ≤ norm 25th percentile: score = 100
  If latency between 25th and 50th percentile: linear from 100 to 75
  If between 50th and 75th: linear from 75 to 50
  If between 75th and 90th: linear from 50 to 25
  If beyond 90th: score = 0–25 based on extremity

Class DLS = mean of per-decision scores
```

Class norms are drawn from the PerformanceVP reference set, refreshed annually based on engagement portfolio data and industry research.

**Overall DLS:**

```
DLS = 0.40 × Operational DLS + 0.35 × Tactical DLS + 0.25 × Strategic DLS
```

The weighting privileges operational latency because it is the leading indicator most directly affecting day-to-day performance.

## 6.6 Reporting

The DLS is reported in the diagnostic report alongside the O composite, with the following content:

- Overall DLS score (0–100)
- Class-level DLS (Operational / Tactical / Strategic)
- Median, 75th and 90th percentile latency per class (in days)
- Identified bottleneck patterns (which approval steps consume disproportionate time)
- Comparison to class norms
- Critical findings (decisions exceeding the 90th percentile latency, or systemic patterns indicating governance failure)

The DLS does NOT enter the P calculation but is one of the most-cited findings in client engagements because it produces a concrete, hard-to-argue number that gives leadership a specific operational target.

## 6.7 Operational conversion rule

The consultant maintains the DLP sampling log in the workbook's "DLP" tab:

| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| Decision identifier | |
| Decision class | |
| Problem identified date | |
| Authorised date | |
| Latency (days) | Computed |
| Per-decision score | 0–100, per the scoring rules |
| Bottleneck flag | |
| Outcome quality | |

**DLS calculation:**

The workbook automatically computes class-level DLS (mean of per-decision scores within each class) and overall DLS (weighted across classes).

### Worked example

A Claims Processing unit of 150 FTE. DLP baseline sample: 20 operational, 15 tactical, 8 strategic decisions.

**Operational decisions (norm: median 3 days, 90th percentile 14 days):**

| Latency days | Count |
|---|---|
| 0–3 days | 8 (40%) → score 100 |
| 4–7 days | 7 (35%) → scores 85–60 |
| 8–14 days | 4 (20%) → scores 50–25 |
| >14 days | 1 (5%) → score 10 |

Operational DLS = (8 × 100 + 7 × 72 + 4 × 38 + 1 × 10) / 20 = (800 + 504 + 152 + 10) / 20 = 73.3

**Tactical decisions (norm: median 12 days, 90th percentile 35 days):**

| Latency | Count | Mean score |
|---|---|---|
| Various | 15 | 58 |

Tactical DLS = 58

**Strategic decisions (norm: median 60 days, 90th percentile 150 days):**

| Latency | Count | Mean score |
|---|---|---|
| Various | 8 | 42 |

Strategic DLS = 42

**Overall DLS:** 0.40 × 73.3 + 0.35 × 58 + 0.25 × 42 = 29.3 + 20.3 + 10.5 = 60.1

**Unit DLS = 60.1, Tier 3 DLP, Confidence: High.**

Identified bottlenecks: tactical decisions are slowed primarily by sequential rather than parallel approval routing; strategic decisions show evidence of unclear authority for sign-off above a certain threshold.

Critical findings: 1 operational decision exceeded 14 days (a customer accommodation that should have been resolved in <3); 2 strategic decisions exceeded 150 days (both involving cross-functional capital allocation).

The diagnostic narrative would surface DLS prominently — it's a hard quantitative finding that maps directly to recommended interventions in O1 (decision rights) and governance design.

## 6.8 Reporting threshold and confidence

Per-class DLS requires minimum sample size: 10 operational, 5 tactical, 3 strategic.

Confidence per the cadence master:
- Operational class: High for 3 months, Medium at 4, Low at 6 (refreshed quarterly)
- Tactical class: High for 6 months, Medium at 8, Low at 12
- Strategic class: High for 12 months, Medium at 14, Low at 18

# Part 7 — Sector Classification (Metadata Only)

The earlier E (System Efficiency) coefficient has been removed from the equation and is **not** replaced by an external sector benchmark. Sector conditions are deliberately kept outside the Performance Equation: P measures the internal, human-and-systemic determinants of performance, and external context is not allowed to distort the internal score. The previously planned Annual Sector Reference (a public-data benchmark overlay compiled from ABS, RBA, Productivity Commission, OBPR and IBISWorld sources) has been **retired**. No external data subscriptions are required, and no sector overlay is applied to P.

Sector classification is retained for one purpose only: as engagement metadata that becomes the substrate for an **internal comparison set** built from PerformanceVP's own accumulating engagement data over time.

## 7.1 Sector classification

For each engagement, the unit is classified along four dimensions:

| Classifier | Source |
|---|---|
| **Sector (ANZSIC division)** | Primary industry classification |
| **Sub-sector (ANZSIC class)** | More granular industry classification |
| **Size band** | <50 FTE / 50–200 FTE / 200–1,000 FTE / >1,000 FTE (organisation-level, not unit-level) |
| **Unit type** | Operations / Sales / Technology / Support functions / Professional services / R&D / Other |

Classification is recorded in the workbook's "Engagement Metadata" tab. It is captured once at engagement setup and re-confirmed at the annual refresh only if the unit's business mix has materially changed. It is metadata, not a measured score, and carries no confidence-decay rule.

## 7.2 Internal comparison set

PerformanceVP's internal comparison set accumulates from the engagement portfolio. Each unit assessed contributes (with appropriate anonymisation and client consent):

| Field | Captured |
|---|---|
| Sector (ANZSIC) | |
| Sub-sector | |
| Size band | |
| Unit type | |
| Unit FTE | |
| Engagement date | |
| P score | |
| C, M, O, S component scores | |
| Sub-dimension scores | |
| DLS | |
| Trip-wire flags (anonymised) | |

The set grows by 5–20 unit observations per engagement. Until enough comparable units accumulate (a working threshold of n ≥ 15 in the same sector × unit-type × size-band cell), reports do **not** make a comparison claim; the unit's P score is presented on its own terms — against the absolute 0–100 colour bands, and (for tracking clients) against the unit's own history. Once a cell is sufficiently populated, reports may note the unit's position within PerformanceVP's own comparison set (e.g. "a P of 64 sits at the Nth percentile of comparable operations units in PerformanceVP's dataset"). This is an internal, real-data comparison — never a public-data external benchmark.

Legal review of data ownership, anonymisation and aggregation rights is required before the comparison set is used in client-facing reporting (see Strategy Part 5.3).

## 7.3 Operational conversion rule

There is no score to compute. The consultant records the four classifiers in the workbook's "Sector Classification" tab as metadata. Where the internal comparison set holds sufficient comparable units, the workbook surfaces the unit's percentile position within that set for optional inclusion in the report; otherwise no comparison is generated and none is reported.

# Part 8 — Triangulation, Gap Rules and Validity

## 8.1 Audit-perception gap rule

For O1, O2 and O3, the rule applies:

```
Gap = |Audit score - Perception score|

If Gap > 15 points (either direction):
  - Both scores reported separately in the diagnostic report
  - The perception score is the value that feeds the O composite (per Diagnostic Workbook Spec 6.6)
  - Pattern noted as a key diagnostic finding
  - Recommended action depends on direction

If Gap ≤ 15 points:
  - Sub-dimension score = mean of the two
  - No special reporting
```

**Interpretation patterns:**

| Pattern | Likely meaning | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Audit high, Perception low | Structure exists on paper but is not experienced as working | Communication and behavioural reinforcement; manager coaching; not structural redesign |
| Audit low, Perception high | Informal workarounds masking structural weakness | Document the workarounds, formalise them, address underlying structural gap |
| Both low | Genuine structural and experiential weakness | Structural redesign + change management |
| Both high | Genuine strength | Maintain; no immediate action |

## 8.2 Survey-behavioural gap rule

For M1, the rule applies:

```
Gap = |M1 survey score - M1 behavioural composite|

If Gap > 15 points:
  - Both scores reported separately
  - Pattern noted as a key diagnostic finding
  - Recommended action depends on direction
```

**Interpretation patterns:**

| Pattern | Likely meaning | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Survey high, Behavioural low | Response acquiescence or social desirability; people say they're engaged but turnover/absenteeism contradicts | Investigate specific sub-populations; review survey administration; check for response pressure |
| Survey low, Behavioural high | Disengaged compliance; people don't endorse engagement on survey but stay and perform | Investigate retention drivers; protect against future degradation; check exit-intent signals |

## 8.3 False-consensus pattern flag

Where TSI3-01 and TSI3-02 (the productive disagreement items) score below 60 AND M2 psychological safety scores above 75, the false-consensus pattern is flagged:

```
If (task conflict items < 60) AND (M2 > 75):
  - Flag as "False consensus pattern" diagnostic finding
  - Interpretation: apparent psychological safety masks suppressed disagreement
  - Recommendation: investigate whether group norms penalise contested debate even though they don't penalise dissent in general; coach leaders on inviting and rewarding productive disagreement
```

This pattern is empirically meaningful — it typically appears in units with strong cohesion norms that have shaded into conformity, and where decision quality is suffering without leaders recognising why.

## 8.4 Trip-wire critical-finding logic

```
For each trip-wire (TW1 Pay equity, TW2 Fairness, TW3 Basic conditions):
  If score < 60:
    - Flag as CRITICAL FINDING in executive summary
    - Escalate to engagement lead and client sponsor
    - Recommend immediate investigation independent of overall M and P scores
    - Document in methodology footer
```

Critical findings are reported regardless of how strong the rest of the unit's performance metrics look. A unit with high P and a failing trip-wire receives a critical finding flag prominently in the executive summary.

## 8.5 Validity and exclusion rules

**Response validity (survey).** Individual responses are excluded if:
- Reverse-scored items agree in direction with forward items on the same sub-dimension (straight-lining)
- Survey completion time below 5th percentile for the cohort (speed-clicking)
- Response shows literal patterning across all items (all 1s, all 5s, alternating)

Excluded responses are logged and reported as percentage of total in the methodology footer.

**Reporting thresholds.** As specified per sub-dimension:
- 60% response rate per unit for sub-dimension reporting
- 4 valid respondents per team and 70% response per team for CII team-level scores
- 12 valid respondents per unit for pulse reporting
- 8 valid respondents per unit for half-yearly reporting
- 75% FTE coverage for Tier 1/2 system data

Below threshold: sub-dimension reported as "insufficient data" and weight reallocated proportionally to remaining sub-dimensions.

**Data vintage rules.** As specified per sub-dimension; sub-dimensions with data older than the "Low confidence" threshold are excluded from composite scoring entirely.

## 8.6 Confidence and decay

Per the Sub-Dimension × Cadence Master Reference Part 7, each sub-dimension carries a confidence band:

| Band | Condition |
|---|---|
| **High** | Within the sub-dimension's high-confidence window |
| **Medium** | Overdue but within 50% of cadence window |
| **Low** | Overdue by more than 50% of cadence window |
| **Excluded** | Beyond the maximum allowed staleness |

The unit P score inherits the lowest confidence band among its contributing sub-dimensions, with explicit annotation in the methodology footer:

```
P score confidence: [High / Medium / Low]
Carry-forward measurements in use: [list of sub-dimensions]
```

This protects the model from false-precision criticism and gives the consultant clear language for explaining why the dashboard shows what it shows.

# Part 9 — Composite Scoring and P Calculation

## 9.1 Sub-dimension scoring

Per sub-dimension, the score is calculated by the rules in Parts 2–7 above. The workbook captures inputs and applies formulas; the consultant verifies the result against the methodology.

**Standard output per sub-dimension:**
- Score (0–100)
- Tier used (1 / 2 / 3 / Mixed)
- Source identifier(s)
- Vintage
- Confidence band (High / Medium / Low)
- Validity exclusions (% excluded, reasons)
- Any gap flags

## 9.2 Component scoring (C, M, O)

The component composites apply the within-component sub-dimension weights specified per industry archetype:

```
C = 0.35(C1) + 0.15(C2) + 0.20(C3) + 0.20(C4) + 0.10(C5)
M = 0.50(M1) + 0.20(M2) + 0.15(M3) + 0.15(M4)
O = 0.30(O1) + 0.25(O2) + 0.20(O3) + 0.10(O4) + 0.15(O5)
```

Adjusted per industry archetype where applicable (the workbook's "Composite Scoring" tab carries the archetype lookup). Note: these are the *within-component* sub-dimension weights. They are distinct from the *component-level* weights (α, β, γ) applied in the P calculation at Part 9.4 below.

**Missing sub-dimension handling.** Where a sub-dimension is reported as "insufficient data" or "excluded", its weight is reallocated proportionally:

```
For component X with sub-dimensions {a, b, c, d, e} and weights {w_a, w_b, w_c, w_d, w_e}:

If sub-dimension c is missing:
  Effective weight for each remaining sub-dimension =
    Original weight / (1 - w_c)

  e.g. if w_c was 0.20 and the remaining weights are 0.35, 0.15, 0.20, 0.10:
  Adjusted weights = 0.4375, 0.1875, 0.25, 0.125 (summing to 1.0)
```

The methodology footer documents the omission and adjustment.

## 9.3 Synergy coefficient

```
S_internal = 0.30(S1) + 0.40(S2) + 0.30(S3)

S = 0.85 + (S_internal / 100) × 0.30
```

If all three Synergy sub-dimensions are unmeasurable, S defaults to 1.00 (neutral) with a "Synergy not measured" annotation.

## 9.4 P score calculation

The P score uses the weighted geometric form, with standardised component weights α (Capability) = 0.35, β (Motivation) = 0.40, γ (Opportunity) = 0.25, which sum to 1:

```
P = S × (C^0.35 × M^0.40 × O^0.25)
```

The unit P score is computed on the 0–100 scale, with the Synergy coefficient applied as a multiplier of the weighted geometric mean of C, M, O. Because the exponents sum to 1, the weighted geometric mean returns a value on the same 0–100 scale as its inputs; the weights tilt the gradient between components (motivation weighted highest, then capability, then opportunity) without letting any single component dominate, and the multiplicative weakest-link logic is preserved. The component weights are standardised across all engagements and are not varied by industry.

**Note on the simplified public form.** For the book, board papers and marketing, the equation is presented in its equal-weighted structural form P = S × (C × M × O)^(1/3) — the special case where α = β = γ = 1/3. The weighted form above is the analytical engine that produces the actual scores; the difference between the two is second-order because the geometric structure dominates in both. See the Strategy document Part 1 for the full treatment.

**Worked example synthesis (continuing from earlier worked examples):**

A hypothetical Customer Service unit with:
- C = 72 (from C1 76.1, C2 73, C3 66, C4 71, C5 79 with default weights)
- M = 70 (from M1 72, M2 75, M3 67, M4 60 with default weights)
- O = 62 (from O1 61.2, O2 65, O3 60, O4 58, O5 66 with default weights)
- S_internal = 56 → S = 0.85 + (56/100) × 0.30 = 1.018

Weighted geometric mean = C^0.35 × M^0.40 × O^0.25 = 72^0.35 × 70^0.40 × 62^0.25.
In logs: 0.35·ln72 + 0.40·ln70 + 0.25·ln62 = 0.35(4.2767) + 0.40(4.2485) + 0.25(4.1271) = 1.4968 + 1.6994 + 1.0318 = 4.2280; exp(4.2280) = 68.5.

P = 1.018 × 68.5 = 69.7

**Unit P = 69.7, with Synergy applied as +1.8% amplification.**

Interpretation: the unit operates at a reasonable performance level overall, slightly above the weighted geometric mean of its components due to mild positive Synergy. The weighted mean (68.5) sits just above the equal-weighted mean (67.9) because the unit's highest-weighted component (Motivation, β=0.40) is stronger than its lowest-weighted component (Opportunity, γ=0.25). The binding constraint is identified by the realistic-P-gain ranking the Diagnostic Workbook implements (Workbook Spec Part 10). Each of the 14 C, M and O sub-dimensions is scored on three quantities: a realistic improvement Δs = ρ × MAX(0, S_cap − score) bounded by diminishing returns (ρ = 0.30, S_cap = 85); the resulting realistic gain in P, ΔP = P × ((1 + w_norm × Δs / compScore)^exponent − 1), which preserves leverage; and a relative-weakness factor rel = 1 / (1 + EXP((score − unitMean) / τ)), τ = 8, that weights each candidate by how far it sits below the unit's own average (here 67.8). Priority = ΔP × rel, and Synergy is excluded as a coefficient. For this unit the ranking leads with O1 Clarity and decision rights (score 61.2), the strongest realistic gain among the sub-dimensions where the unit is genuinely below its own level. The top six by Priority: O1 (0.41), M4 (0.32), O3 (0.30), M1 (0.29), O2 (0.25), C3 (0.21). M1 Engagement and confidence is the instructive case: it carries the largest realistic P gain of any sub-dimension (ΔP ≈ 0.77, reflecting its 0.50 weight in the heaviest component), yet it ranks fourth, because at 72 it sits above the unit's own average and the relative-weakness factor discounts it. That is the method working as intended, since a high-leverage strength should not be mistaken for a constraint. The binding component, the lowest of C, M and O, is Opportunity, consistent with the O1 headline. The binding-constraint statement leads with O1; intervention priority: clarity and decision rights.

## 9.5 Carry-forward conventions

For Tracking Subscription engagements where the P score is recalculated between full refreshes:

- Each sub-dimension uses its most recent valid measurement
- Sub-dimensions not refreshed in the current cycle are carried forward with their previous value
- The confidence band tracks the date of measurement, not the date of P recalculation
- The methodology footer notes which sub-dimensions are carried forward and from what date

The P score recalculates at the half-yearly health check and the annual baseline refresh — the two points at which genuinely new survey and scoring data enters the equation — using these conventions. The quarterly pulse does NOT trigger a P recalculation (insufficient new data); it operates as an early-warning trajectory layer only. An event-triggered refresh can also recalculate P out of cycle where a defined trigger justifies it (see the Sub-Dimension × Cadence Master Reference).

## 9.6 Methodology footer requirements

Every diagnostic report and dashboard view includes a methodology footer. The workbook auto-populates this from inputs across all tabs. Required content:

**Engagement metadata:**
- Client, unit name, FTE
- Sector classification (ANZSIC, sub-sector, size band, unit type)
- Industry archetype applied
- Engagement / refresh date

**Tier mix:**
- Per sub-dimension: tier used, source identifier, vintage
- Composite tier rating ("Tier 1 dominant" / "Tier 2 dominant" / "Tier 3 dominant" / "Mixed")

**Validity and exclusions:**
- Response rates per survey deployed
- % of responses excluded for validity reasons (straight-lining etc.)
- Sub-dimensions excluded for insufficient data
- Weight reallocation applied

**Confidence:**
- Per sub-dimension confidence band
- Overall P score confidence band
- Carry-forward measurements (if any), with originating dates

**Gap flags:**
- Audit-perception gaps fired (which sub-dimensions, magnitude, interpretation)
- Survey-behavioural gaps fired
- False-consensus pattern flag fired

**Critical findings:**
- Trip-wire failures
- Other escalation-grade findings

**Internal comparison vintage:**
- Size and composition of PerformanceVP's internal comparison set applied (sector × unit-type × size-band cell, n), or a note that no comparison was applied because the cell is not yet sufficiently populated
- No external/public-data sector benchmark is applied (the Annual Sector Reference has been retired)

This methodology footer is the audit trail that protects the offering against "we don't know how this number was calculated" criticism. It is part of every client deliverable.
